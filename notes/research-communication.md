## 2. Communication with the public: the dashboard

The dashboard at https://epiforecasts.io/covid/ is still online and lists Sam Abbott and Joel Hellewell as lead authors from CMMID, with global, regional, national and subnational estimates.
The site ran from around April 2020 to 31 March 2022, "more than 2 years after we published the first set of estimates" (rt-reflections, line 31).
Scale: "we have estimated reproduction numbers daily for several thousand locations" (rt-reflections, line 4).
User numbers as of March 2022: "just over 500 thousand unique users since April 2020 with 1.2 million page views (with 800 thousand of these being for our US estimates, 120 thousand for our global estimates, and only 20 thousand for our methods)" (rt-reflections, line 46).
User numbers as of January 2023: "our platform ended the pandemic with > 1 million unique users" (IISA post, line 58).
The "million users" line should be sourced to the January 2023 post; the March 2022 post gives 500 thousand unique users and 1.2 million page views.
Twenty thousand of 1.2 million page views were for the methods page, which is a usable fact about what the public read.
Stated aim: "to provide both decision makers and the general public with real-time information on how the epidemic was progressing" (rt-reflections, line 35).
Partners and funding: Microsoft AI for Health compute grant and technical help from the UK Met Office (rt-reflections, line 65).
Estimates "were processed regularly by various national international organisations such as the WHO, especially in the early parts of the epidemic" (rt-reflections, line 48).
What was hard, in his words: "Because we did not have the capacity to manually inspect the data and estimates on a daily basis we sometimes published nonsensical estimates. Lack of capacity also meant that we struggled to explain how these came about and this caused some consternation amongst visitors to the web site" (rt-reflections, line 63).
On democratised methods: "this can cause issues if the limitations of the method are poorly communicated" (rt-reflections, line 57).
On uncertainty: "it can be difficult to fully incorporate uncertainty from all steps of the analysis pipeline into subsequent steps" (rt-reflections, line 57).
His own do's and don'ts for communicating with the public and stakeholders (IISA post, lines 146-151):
"Be as open as possible."
"Always clearly state the limitations of your work (ideally first)."
"No point estimates!"
"Technical detail needs to be present but often not in the main report (just list the main assumptions and limitations in plain text)."
"Joint statements across teams are very useful for public facing work as generally there is a broad consensus with different views on the details. If results are presented individually this can get lost and it incentivises being first out (and maybe therefore cutting corners)."
"I personally didn't feel it was useful for me to directly interact with the public/media. No training. No time. Explaining the evidence not my speciality leave it to others for whom that is a speciality."
On social media as a channel: "I routinely share my research, and summaries of others research on twitter (where I have over 2k+ followers)" (promotion post, line 38).
The PPIE deck opens with a Hans Rosling quote from Liberia 2014: "We were losing ourselves in details [...] all we needed to know is, are the number of cases rising, falling or levelling off?" (`/Users/lshsa2/code/seabbs/hpru-ppie-kickoff/slides.qmd:12-14`).
The PPIE deck asks the public: "What concerns do you have about 'black box' combined predictions?" and "How can we build trust when the ensemble disagrees with individual expert models?" (`slides.qmd:158-160`).
It also asks: "How do we communicate when we've chosen less detail for better certainty?" (`slides.qmd:170`).
It cites a human judgement forecasting challenge where a "Public/researcher ensemble outperformed models in some settings; combined ensemble of all forecasts performed best" (`slides.qmd:93-97`).
PPIE plan: each PhD student consults the panel twice per analysis, with Bluesky engagement and a summary article with panel feedback (`slides.qmd:111-128`).
He is PPIE lead for the Forecasting and Scenario Modelling theme of the NIHR HPRU (`/Users/lshsa2/code/epiforecasts/bmbr-2025-outbreak-methods/application/application.qmd:387`).

### LLM-isms he strips from his own writing
The catalogue is `/Users/lshsa2/code/seabbs/JuliaCon2026/notes/llmisms.md`, mined from the git history of three decks, with eighteen patterns.
Rule: "The default rewrite is deletion, not rephrasing" (line 26).
Most removed patterns by count: the bolted-on clause (a trailing which/and/so idea the sentence did not need) and the moral (a closing line stating significance rather than a fact) (lines 33-34).
Others: the point-stamp (a clause saying the previous sentence mattered), the slogan callout, rule of three, negative parallelism ("not X but Y"), title echo, the colon gloss, overclaimed identity ("really is", "exactly", "just"), persuasive adjectives ("comprehensive, powerful, seamless, robust, novel"), metadiscourse, room flattery, and unsupported precision (lines 38-656).
Unsupported precision is "the failure the author polices hardest", citing a commit correcting 41 claims the git record did not support (lines 653-656).
What survives editing: "Blunt admissions against interest", sourced numbers, and "Attribution and citation lines. These get longer, not shorter, over the history" (lines 929-943).

## 5. What the workflow paper says about communication

Manuscript: `/Users/lshsa2/code/seabbs/a-workflow-for-infectious-disease-modelling/main.tex`.
He is section lead for "Outbreak Evolution and Workflow Iteration" and "Reporting" (lines 595-610).
"Where barriers to transparent reporting exist, it is always better to report as much as possible" (line 619).
"We recommend sharing code and data alongside results because it enables verification, accelerates progress, and is just as much a part of the analysis as the manuscript" (line 616).
"Making analyses public at all stages builds trust, though confidential data requires careful management" (line 615).
"We also recommend visual schematics of the workflow and model architecture are developed and referred to throughout the reporting process" (line 621).
"Without structured reporting, even well-designed workflows are challenging to assess or improve" (line 613).
Decision makers: "we need both short-term forecasts for operational planning and effective reproduction number estimates for public communication and interpretability. Our aim is to translate these policy needs into specific target estimands, by understanding how parameters inform decisions" (line 198).
Stakeholders: "These limitations help shape stakeholder expectations and prevent misuse of results" (line 199).
"Seeing model output often helps communicate assumptions to non-modellers more effectively than written specifications" (line 527).
Ensembles can be "less timely and/or less precise, with lower utility for decision makers than the component models" (line 582).
It cites Behrend et al. 2020 on "stakeholder engagement, reproducibility, data documentation, uncertainty communication, and testable model outcomes" (line 1026).
Software practice: unit testing, continuous integration and version control "to track model evolution" (line 468).
